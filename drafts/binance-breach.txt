# The $6.3 Billion Binance Heist: How Hackers Pulled Off One of Crypto's Biggest Thefts

*An inside look at the security breach, the technology exploited, where the stolen money went, and what it means for cryptocurrency security.*

**Date:** 31 October 2026  
**Reading time:** 5 minutes

## 1. What Happened?

On 29 October 2026, Binance, the world's largest cryptocurrency exchange, suffered a major security breach that resulted in approximately **$6.36 billion in cryptocurrency being stolen**.

According to blockchain intelligence firm TRM Labs, the attackers moved funds across ten blockchain networks, with most of the theft occurring within approximately 20 minutes.

For perspective, the infamous Bybit hack in February 2025 involved approximately $1.5 billion. The Binance incident is more than four times larger.

However, this wasn't a conventional scam involving fake websites or stolen customer passwords. It appears to have been a sophisticated attack on the infrastructure responsible for authorizing cryptocurrency transactions.

## 2. How Did the Attack Work?

Cryptocurrency exchanges generally maintain two types of wallets:

- **Cold wallets:** Kept offline or isolated from operational systems for long-term storage.
- **Hot wallets:** Connected to operational infrastructure and used for everyday deposits and withdrawals.

Hot wallets require private keys to authorize transactions. These keys are typically protected using specialized signing infrastructure.

Initial investigations suggested that attackers compromised a laptop belonging to a Binance DevOps engineer and subsequently gained access to internal infrastructure. Binance later indicated that the attack involved multiple breach points.

The most important part of the attack was reportedly the compromise of systems responsible for transaction authorization.

Here's why that matters.

Imagine a bank vault with an extremely secure lock. Instead of breaking the lock, an attacker compromises the control room that authorizes access to the vault.

The attacker may never need to physically steal the key.

A similar principle applies to cryptocurrency signing systems. If an attacker compromises a service authorized to request cryptographic signatures, they may be able to initiate unauthorized transactions without directly extracting the underlying private keys.

### The Ethereum EIP-7702 connection

One particularly interesting technical detail identified by TRM Labs involved Ethereum's EIP-7702 functionality.

EIP-7702 allows externally owned accounts (EOAs) to delegate certain functionality to smart-contract code.

Investigators reported that attackers abused this mechanism to turn a Binance hot wallet into an attacker-controlled sweeper.

A sweeper automatically transfers funds from one wallet to another.

The attackers reportedly prepared 50 pre-signed authorizations in advance, suggesting considerable planning before the actual theft.

Importantly, Binance stated that investigators had found no evidence of wallet private keys being leaked.

## 3. Where Did the $6.36 Billion Go?

Stealing cryptocurrency is only half the challenge. Converting it into usable money without getting caught is considerably harder.

Blockchain transactions are publicly visible, allowing investigators to track stolen funds across wallets and networks.

A significant portion of the stolen assets consisted of stablecoins such as USDT and USDC.

Unlike Bitcoin and Ethereum, stablecoins issued by centralized companies have an important feature: their issuers can freeze tokens at specific addresses.

Following the incident, companies including Tether, Circle and Ripple cooperated with investigators to restrict affected assets.

Approximately **$3 billion worth of assets were reportedly frozen or immobilized**.

The attackers also attempted to convert stablecoins into ETH.

One reported transaction involved converting approximately $325 million in stablecoins into around $285 million worth of ETH.

Why would attackers willingly lose $40 million?

Because ETH cannot be directly frozen by a centralized token issuer. An attacker might prefer $285 million in an asset they can control over $325 million that could be frozen.

This demonstrates an important distinction in cryptocurrency: the value of an asset and the ability to control that asset are not necessarily the same thing.

## 4. Who Was Behind the Attack?

Blockchain intelligence firm TRM Labs attributed the incident to TraderTraitor, a hacking group associated with North Korea.

Other investigations have also identified similarities with previous North Korean-linked cryptocurrency thefts.

North Korean hacking groups have been associated with several major attacks, including the $1.5 billion Bybit theft in February 2025.

Their techniques have included:

- Compromising employee devices.
- Social engineering.
- Exploiting internal infrastructure.
- Targeting transaction-signing systems.
- Moving stolen funds across multiple blockchain networks.

However, attribution by blockchain intelligence firms is not the same as an official confirmation of responsibility. Binance has not publicly confirmed the attackers' identities.

## 5. How Is Binance Handling the Losses?

Binance announced that it would absorb the financial impact rather than pass the losses to customers.

It committed to using its Secure Asset Fund for Users (SAFU) and corporate treasury.

SAFU is an emergency reserve established by Binance in 2018 to provide additional protection for users.

The company also announced additional security checks and an updated Proof of Reserves report.

These developments raise an important distinction:

**Recovering stolen funds and compensating customers are two different things.**

Even if attackers retain some of the stolen cryptocurrency, Binance can compensate affected customers using its own reserves.

However, independently verifiable financial disclosures remain important for establishing the strength of those assurances.

Source: [Binance – Official Security Incident Update](https://www.binance.com/en/support/announcement/detail/d3306d9dca5243548c813b605e1d1751)

## 6. Five Security Lessons From the Incident

The Binance breach highlights several important cybersecurity principles.

**1. Protect transaction authorization, not just private keys.** Even if private keys remain secure, compromised services authorized to request signatures can create serious risks.

**2. Follow the principle of least privilege.** Employees and internal services should only have access to the systems necessary for their responsibilities.

**3. Introduce transaction-level safeguards.** Large withdrawals should require additional authorization, independent verification and risk checks.

**4. Monitor blockchain activity continuously.** Real-time monitoring can help exchanges detect suspicious transactions before attackers move funds further.

**5. Secure internal infrastructure.** Employee laptops, cloud services, deployment pipelines and internal containers are all part of an exchange's security perimeter.

## 7. The Bigger Picture

Does this mean blockchain technology has failed?

Not necessarily.

Bitcoin and Ethereum are decentralized networks. Binance is a centralized company that provides trading, custody and withdrawal services on top of those networks.

When users deposit cryptocurrency into Binance, they generally surrender direct control of the associated private keys. The exchange becomes responsible for protecting and managing those assets.

The blockchain can continue functioning perfectly even when an exchange's internal infrastructure is compromised.

Interestingly, blockchain transparency also helped investigators track the stolen funds and coordinate with stablecoin issuers to freeze approximately $3 billion.

But transparency does not guarantee recovery, and decentralization does not eliminate operational risk.

The Binance incident reinforces one fundamental principle of cryptocurrency security:

**Protecting the private key is only half the job. Protecting every system that has the authority to use that key is equally important.**
